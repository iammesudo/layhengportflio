ClimateHot season note · Four minute read

Keeping a house cool without more air-con

Most new houses in the city are built to be cooled by machines: big windows facing the sun, low ceilings, sealed rooms. The air conditioner then fights the building all day. Older houses here did it the other way round.

Shade first

Deep eaves, verandas, screens and trees keep the sun off the walls and the glass. A window in shade lets in light without the heat. This is the cheapest cooling there is, and it works when the power is off.

Air that moves through

Rooms lined up with the breeze, windows on two sides, high vents and a court or light well to pull warm air up and out. A ceiling fan over every bed and sofa does the rest for most of the year.

Walls that breathe

Lime plaster lets damp walls dry out instead of trapping water behind paint. Thick walls hold the cool of the night into the morning.

The air conditioner stays, for the hottest weeks and the bedrooms at night. It just stops being the first tool.

How terrazzo is poured on site

Terrazzo is one of the few floors that is made where it lies. It is cool underfoot, it can be polished again in twenty years, and it can carry chips of stone left over from other jobs.

  1. We choose the chips with you: marble, local stone, a little glass, sometimes broken tile. Samples are poured in the studio first.
  2. The chips are mixed with grey or white cement and colour, and poured between thin strips that divide the floor.
  3. The floor cures slowly and is kept damp for days, so it does not crack.
  4. Then it is ground flat, filled, and polished in several passes, finer each time.

We pour in the dry months when we can. Rain and high humidity slow the curing, and a floor that dries unevenly shows it for ever.

WorkshopRainy season note · Three minute read

A morning at the joinery workshop

Most of our furniture is made in a workshop on the edge of the city, by people we have worked with for years. We bring drawings in Khmer and English, and we change them at the bench when the makers know better.

Dry wood in a wet country

Wood moves with the air. A table made from wet timber will crack or twist in its first rainy season. We use reclaimed hardwood that has dried for years, or plywood with a solid edge where a piece must stay flat.

Oil, not lacquer

Lacquer looks perfect on the first day and peels in the humidity. Oil soaks in, darkens slowly, and can be sanded and oiled again at home. We leave a small bottle with every piece.

And when something breaks, the same workshop comes back to fix it.
