A rule from the author

“The insect itself is not to be drawn. It is not even to be seen from a distance.”

Kafka in a letter to his publisher, 25 October 1915, about the cover of the first edition.

This site keeps to it. Gregor is never shown. Only the page changes: as you read, the paper darkens, the type grows heavy and the walls of the column move in.The novella has three parts. So does this page.

I

Part one

The door

“What’s happened to me?” he thought. It wasn’t a dream.

Gregor Samsa is a travelling salesman. He wakes up changed, and late for his train. The chief clerk from his firm comes to the flat to ask why. Through the door, Gregor’s voice no longer sounds human, and the door is locked from the inside. Scroll to turn the key.

“set himself to the task of turning the key in the lock with his mouth.”The key · 1 of 4

“Listen”, said the chief clerk in the next room, “he’s turning the key.”The key · 2 of 4

“The clear sound of the lock as it snapped back was Gregor’s sign that he could break his concentration”The lock · 3 of 4

“Because he had to open the door in this way, it was already wide open before he could be seen.”The door · 4 of 4

His room, a proper human room although a little too small, lay peacefully between its four familiar walls. A collection of textile samples lay spread out on the table – Samsa was a travelling salesman – and above it there hung a picture that he had recently cut out of an illustrated magazine and housed in a nice, gilded frame. It showed a lady fitted out with a fur hat and fur boa who sat upright, raising a heavy fur muff that covered the whole of her lower arm towards the viewer.

II

Part two

The room

“They were emptying his room out; taking away everything that was dear to him…”

Gregor now lives behind a closed door. His sister Grete brings his food. When she sees that he likes to crawl over the walls and ceiling, she and their mother start to carry the furniture out to give him room. His mother is afraid it will look as if they have given up hope. Scroll to empty the room.

“but all the furniture apart from the all-important couch.”

“they had already taken out the chest containing his fretsaw and other tools;”

“now they threatened to remove the writing desk with its place clearly worn into the floor”

“This picture at least, now totally covered by Gregor, would certainly be taken away by no-one.”

Had he really wanted to transform his room into a cave, a warm room fitted out with the nice furniture he had inherited? That would have let him crawl around unimpeded in any direction, but it would also have let him quickly forget his past when he had still been human. He had come very close to forgetting, and it had only been the voice of his mother, unheard for so long, that had shaken him out of it.

III

Part three

The violin

“Was he an animal if music could captivate him so?”

“No-one dared to remove the apple lodged in Gregor’s flesh, so it remained there as a visible reminder of his injury.” The family take jobs and let a room to three gentlemen. One evening Grete plays the violin for them in the living room, and Gregor, drawn by the music, crawls out to listen.

Yet Gregor’s sister was playing so beautifully. Her face was leant to one side, following the lines of music with a careful and melancholy expression. Gregor crawled a little further forward, keeping his head close to the ground so that he could meet her eyes if the chance came. Was he an animal if music could captivate him so? It seemed to him that he was being shown the way to the unknown nourishment he had been yearning for.

After

The last pages

The tram

“They had the tram, filled with warm sunshine, all to themselves.”

Gregor is found in the morning by the cleaner. That same day his parents and Grete leave the flat together, something they had not done for months, and take the tram out to the open country.

“as soon as they reached their destination Grete was the first to get up and

stretch out

her young body.”

About the book

Franz Kafka (3 July 1883 to 3 June 1924) was a German-language writer from Prague.

Die Verwandlung was first published in October 1915 in the journal Die weißen Blätter. The first edition as a book appeared in December 1915 in the series Der jüngste Tag, edited by Kurt Wolff. It is told in three parts.

The first English translation, by Willa and Edwin Muir, appeared in 1933. The passages here are from David Wyllie’s translation.
